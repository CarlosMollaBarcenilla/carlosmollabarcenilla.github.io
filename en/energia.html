On this page I present two projects that tackle this challenge from different angles: storing surplus renewable energy in Carnot batteries and how the electricity market works.

Project 01

CO₂ Carnot Batteries

Master's thesis, University of Liège

Storing surplus renewable energy as heat and converting it back into electricity. My work focused on the discharge stage: a transcritical CO₂ power cycle that I modelled, optimised and analysed in Python, and defended in English in June 2026.

“Part-Load Performance Analysis of a Transcritical CO₂ Low-Temperature Power Cycle”

  • 20.3 %maximum net cycle efficiency
  • 10 MWnet design power

The problem

The high intermittency of renewables

Solar and wind generation depends on the weather, not on demand. This creates mismatches between production and consumption: at peak generation hours there are surpluses that, without storage capacity, have to be curtailed, while in low-production periods the power system has to rely on backup technologies.

Energy storage is therefore a key piece of the energy transition. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), around 235 GW of storage was installed worldwide at the end of 2023, while reaching the net zero emissions scenario would require 1,500 GW by 2030.

Carnot batteries are a long-duration energy storage (LDES) solution: they convert electricity into heat, store it in thermal tanks and turn it back into electricity when the grid needs it, without relying on critical raw materials such as lithium.

  • No critical raw materialsIt uses abundant, non-toxic storage media such as water, molten salts or rocks instead of lithium or cobalt.
  • Flexible sitingUnlike pumped hydro, it does not require specific geographical conditions.
  • Long service lifeAround 20 years, compared with less than 10 for most electrochemical batteries, since it does not depend on chemical reactions.
  • Sector couplingBy storing thermal energy, it can supply electricity and useful heat at the same time, for example to district heating networks.
The discharge

The transcritical power cycle

In the discharge stage, the CO₂ follows a transcritical Rankine cycle: it operates above its critical point (31.1 °C and 73.8 bar) on the high-pressure side and below it on the low-pressure side. Selecting each stage highlights its path on the diagram.

  1. 1 → 2 PumpingThe pump raises the pressure of the liquid CO₂ above its critical pressure.
  2. 2 → 3 HeatingThe CO₂ is preheated in the recuperator and absorbs heat from the hot source in a supercritical state, with no phase change.
  3. 3 → 4 ExpansionThe fluid expands in the turbine, which drives the generator and returns the energy to the grid.
  4. 4 → 1 CondensationAfter giving off heat in the recuperator, the CO₂ condenses against the cold source and returns to the pump inlet.

Advantages of the transcritical CO₂ cycle

Thermal matchingIn the supercritical state, the CO₂ heats up with a temperature glide that matches that of the source, which reduces exergy destruction in the heat exchange.
Compact equipmentThe high density of CO₂ near the critical point makes it possible to reduce the size of the turbomachinery and heat exchangers.
Pumping instead of compressionSince the fluid is pressurised in the liquid phase, the work required is much lower than that of a compressor.
Safe and sustainable fluidIt is non-flammable and non-toxic at moderate concentrations, with zero ozone depletion potential and a global warming potential (GWP) of 1.
Methodology

From the model to the control strategy

  1. Cycle model

    I developed in Python a steady-state model of the recuperated cycle, using the University of Liège's LaboThApPy library and CoolProp for the CO₂ properties. The heat exchangers were discretised to capture the strong non-linearity of the specific heat near the critical point.

  2. Design optimisation

    Using a particle swarm optimisation (PSO) algorithm, I identified, for each pair of hot (130–200 °C) and cold (0.1–20 °C) source temperatures, the operating point with maximum net efficiency, at a fixed net power of 10 MW.

  3. Off-design analysis

    With the equipment geometry fixed at the design point, I analysed the cycle's response to ±15 % variations in pump and expander speeds and to changes in boundary conditions. Based on this, I proposed a structure of controlled and manipulated variables.

Analysis

Main results

01 / 08

Net cycle efficiency

Efficiency increases with the hot source temperature and decreases with the cold source temperature. The global optimum lies in the most favourable corner: 200 °C and 0.1 °C.

20.3 % maximum net efficiency, around 48 % of the Carnot limit.

02 / 08

Optimal high-side pressure

For each pair of temperatures there is an optimal pressure. Raising it improves the thermal matching with the hot source, but increases the pumping work.

From about 145 bar at 130 °C to about 230 bar at 200 °C.

03 / 08

CO₂ mass flow rate

To maintain a constant net power of 10 MW, the required flow rate varies significantly with the boundary conditions. This range defines the turndown capability required of the pump.

From 161 kg/s at the optimum to about 465 kg/s in the worst case: a factor of three.

04 / 08

Hot source flow rate

The higher the hot source temperature, the lower the flow rate needed to supply the same heat. Modulating it makes it possible to regulate the thermal energy entering the cycle.

From about 80 to 350 kg/s: a factor of 4.4.

05 / 08

Off-design: actuator authority

With the geometry fixed, the pump and expander speeds were varied by ±15 %. The almost horizontal pressure and flow isolines show that both variables depend almost exclusively on the pump.

Pump speed governs the cycle's pressure and flow rate.

06 / 08

Sensitivity to the hot source

Between 135 and 190 °C, efficiency and power increase without changing the actuator set points, while the pressure barely changes (152.7–153.8 bar).

Efficiency from 9.2 % to 14.6 % and power from 7.1 to 13.1 MW.

07 / 08

Sensitivity to the cold source

This is the main disturbance: a warmer heat sink lowers the CO₂ density at the pump inlet, which reduces the flow rate and the turbine expansion ratio.

From 0.1 to 20 °C, efficiency drops from 11.5 % to 9.0 % and power is almost halved, from 10.1 to 5.4 MW.

08 / 08

Diminishing returns of the hot flow rate

Increasing the hot source flow rate raises the power, but with diminishing returns: the heater reaches its heat transfer limit.

From 154 to 173 kg/s, 1.3 MW is gained; from 211 to 231 kg/s, only 0.1 MW. A flow rate higher than necessary is not justified.

1 / 8
Conclusions

Proposed control strategy

  • The high-side pressure is the main controlled variable and is regulated mainly through the pump speed.
  • The cold source temperature is the main disturbance: it should be measured and included as feedforward in the pump control loop.
  • The hot source flow rate and the expander speed act as secondary actuators, affecting efficiency and power but not pressure.
  • The expander speed should be subordinated to the pump speed: increasing it is only beneficial when the flow rate is sufficient.

The study is limited to steady state. Future work includes a dynamic model to validate the strategy in closed loop, for example with model predictive control (MPC), and its coupling with the charging stage to evaluate the round-trip efficiency of the complete system.

Project 02

Electricity market simulator

Personal project

To understand how the price of electricity is set, I developed in Python and Streamlit a web application that simulates the wholesale electricity market in real time. It is designed for training sessions: one person acts as the market operator from a computer and everyone else takes part from their phones as generation companies.

Try the simulator View the code on GitHub
  1. 01The market operator

    Whoever runs the game opens a room on their computer, projects it on screen and shows a QR code. For each hour of the day, they publish the forecast demand and the renewable output.

  2. 02The companies

    Participants scan the QR code with their phones and register their generation company. Each one manages four plants (nuclear, coal, combined cycle and gas) and submits how much power it offers and at what price.

  3. 03Market clearing

    The operator clears the market: bids are ranked from cheapest to most expensive until demand is covered, and the last accepted bid sets the price for all. If demand is not covered, there is a blackout and the round is repeated.

  4. 04The ranking

    After five hours of trading, the company with the highest profit wins, once operating, start-up and shutdown costs and the costs of sudden power changes have been deducted.
