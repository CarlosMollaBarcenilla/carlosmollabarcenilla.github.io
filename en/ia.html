Project 01

Neural network for Pac-Man

Introduction to Artificial Intelligence, University of Liège

I used PyTorch to train a convolutional neural network that learns to play Pac-Man from examples, with no programmed rules. Starting from 15,018 game situations, each with the state of the board and the action taken, the network learns to choose the best move: this is supervised learning.

The video shows real games played by the trained network in mazes that were not part of the training set. It completes each level while dodging the ghost, which shows its ability to generalise.

It is the same principle behind generative AI models such as ChatGPT or Claude: a neural network is not given rules but a large number of examples, and it adjusts its parameters to reduce the error of its predictions. My network learns which move to make from games; a language model learns which word comes next from texts. The scale is very different, but the training logic is the same.

Beyond the game

This same process is the one applied in engineering data analysis: preparing and encoding the data properly, training a model, validating it on cases it has not seen and measuring its results. That is what I want to apply to real problems, such as forecasting energy demand or detecting anomalies in the operation of an installation.

Project 02

Chess engine

Personal project

After the course I wanted to find out whether I could design a complete AI on my own. I chose chess because it is a game I have always loved and because it is a classic decision-making problem: there are too many possible moves to analyse them all, so the AI has to decide what to calculate and what to discard.

I approached it as an engineering project: defining the problem, choosing the algorithm, measuring its performance and debugging until I had a product anyone can use.

How it decides

Alpha-beta pruning search

To choose a move, the AI analyses its options, the opponent's replies and its own counter-replies, and picks the one that guarantees the best outcome even if the opponent plays well.

The challenge is that the number of positions grows exponentially. Alpha-beta pruning discards the branches that can no longer change the decision, and the time saved is spent calculating more moves ahead. It is the same idea as in any optimisation problem: do not evaluate alternatives we already know will not win.

The evaluation

Each piece is worth more on some squares than on others

When the search reaches a final position, the AI scores it: it adds up each side's material plus a bonus depending on the square each piece occupies. A knight in the centre controls eight squares; in a corner, only two.

I use piece-square tables based on the well-known “Simplified Evaluation Function”, in hundredths of a pawn. Select a piece to see its map.
